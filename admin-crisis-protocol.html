Crisis Response Protocol

A consistent safety procedure for Prayer Project administrators reviewing requests that may describe suicide, self-harm, or threats to other people.

This dashboard is not an emergency response center.

A Prayer Project administrator is not acting as a therapist, crisis counselor, dispatcher, or law-enforcement officer. A flagged request may be seen after a delay. Never imply that submitting a prayer request activates emergency intervention.

First: identify the pathway

1
Suicide / self-harm concernUse 988 as the primary U.S. crisis resource. If the message describes immediate life-threatening physical danger, 911 may also be appropriate.
2
Threat to another personIf the message describes an immediate threat of violence, a weapon, or imminent physical danger, the emergency pathway is 911 or the appropriate local emergency number.
3
Both concerns appearTreat immediate physical danger as the highest-priority safety issue while also directing suicide or mental-health crisis needs to 988.

Automated flags are screening signals only. They are not diagnoses, findings of intent, or proof that a threat is genuine.

Five-step suicide-support framework

1
AskIf direct contact is appropriate and available, it is okay to ask clearly whether the person is thinking about suicide.
2
Be thereListen without judgment. Do not argue, shame, preach at, or minimize the person’s distress.
3
Help keep them safeEncourage immediate connection with people and professionals who can help reduce danger. Do not personally attempt unsafe intervention.
4
Help them connectUse 988, a trusted person, a mental-health professional, medical care, or emergency services as appropriate.
5
Follow up when appropriateIf the Prayer Project has a safe, authorized way to follow up, continued supportive contact can matter. Do not promise continuous monitoring.

Administrators must not

  • Promise that The Prayer Project can rescue, locate, monitor, or immediately protect a person.
  • Present an automated crisis flag as a clinical diagnosis or certainty.
  • Promise secrecy when a message describes possible immediate danger.
  • Attempt to provide therapy, diagnose mental illness, or give medical treatment advice.
  • Debate whether someone has a “good enough reason” to feel suicidal.
  • Approve a safety-flagged request onto the public prayer wall merely to make it visible to more people.
  • Share crisis-related information beyond people who need it for safety, moderation, legal, or operational reasons.

Review and documentation

  1. Read the full request in context, not only the matched-term label.
  2. Determine whether the flag appears to concern self-harm, another-person threat, both, or a likely false positive.
  3. Keep the request non-public while a meaningful safety concern remains unresolved.
  4. Use clear, factual notes. Do not speculate about diagnoses, motives, or intent.
  5. If appropriate contact information exists, provide the relevant crisis or emergency resource without claiming that the Prayer Project is monitoring the person.
  6. Review crisis-related records for deletion after 90 days unless they remain necessary for an active moderation, safety, abuse-prevention, legal, or operational reason.

What to communicate

When communicating with someone whose request raises a suicide or self-harm concern, keep the message simple: their safety matters, they do not need to wait for an administrator, 988 is available in the United States, and immediate physical danger belongs with emergency services.

For faith-centered communication, it is appropriate to say that prayer and professional crisis care are not competing choices. Avoid implying that suicidal thoughts reflect weak faith or insufficient prayer.
